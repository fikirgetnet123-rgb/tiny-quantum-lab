Experiment 06 — Entanglement

How can two quantum results stay connected?

Create pairs of entangled photons and send one to each detector. Choose a measurement angle on each side, then compare the results from many pairs. Each result is random on its own, but the two results show a precise pattern when compared.

Try this: set both detector angles equal. How often do the two results match?
0 pairs measured · 0 matching results · 0 different results

What did you notice?

Each result is random. The pattern between them is not.

Each detector records a random “+” or “−” result. But when we compare the results from many entangled pairs, the two lists are strongly correlated. When the detector angles are equal, this model gives matching results. Changing the angle difference changes the pattern in a predictable way.

the important “not magic” note

Entanglement does not let anyone send a message faster than light. Each local result is random, so neither observer can choose the result they want. The correlation becomes visible only when the two observers compare their results using ordinary communication.

The idea, step by step

What makes entanglement different?

  1. 1

    The two particles share one quantum state.

    The pair is described by a shared quantum state. It is not enough to think of each photon as carrying its own complete, independent answer.

  2. 2

    Each result is individually random.

    Before a measurement, there is no reliable way to predict whether one detector will record “+” or “−”.

  3. 3

    The results become meaningful when compared.

    After measuring many pairs, the two observers can compare their records. The fraction of matching and different results follows a pattern controlled by the angle between their detectors.

  4. 4

    Experiments have tested these correlations.

    Bell-test experiments have shown correlations that cannot be explained by theories based on ordinary local hidden instructions. The predictions of quantum mechanics match the observed pattern.

The essential maths

P(same) = cos²(θL − θR)

In this simplified polarization model, θL − θR is the difference between the two detector angles. When the angles are the same, matching results are most likely. As the angle difference changes, the matching probability changes too.

P(+) = P(−) = 1/2

Looking at either detector by itself gives a random 50/50 result. The interesting information appears in the relationship between the two sets of results.

Make a change, see a reason

What do the controls actually do?

Match the angles

Both detectors make the same measurement. In this model, the results from each pair strongly match.

Use different angles

The detectors are measuring in different directions. The fraction of matching results changes with the angle difference.

Increase pair rate

You collect more measurements in less time. This does not change the probability rule; it just gives you more data.

Compare, do not signal

Neither observer controls their individual result. The correlation only becomes visible after the two records are compared.
