Experiment 07 — Quantum computing

How can a qubit be different from an ordinary bit?

An ordinary bit is 0 or 1. A qubit has a quantum state that can give either result when measured. Build a tiny circuit, then measure the same prepared qubit many times to see the probabilities predicted by that state.

Try this: use the H gate on a qubit in state 0. Before you measure it, what do you expect to see after 100 measurements?
0 measurements · 0 results: 0 · 0 results: 1

What did you notice?

A qubit does not give both answers when you measure it.

Before measurement, a qubit can be in a state that gives probabilities for 0 and 1. The H gate prepares an equal 50/50 state from 0. When you measure it, you get one result: 0 or 1. Repeat the same experiment many times and the results reveal the probabilities of the state.

The idea, step by step

What makes a qubit quantum?

  1. 1

    A normal bit has one value.

    It is 0 or it is 1. Reading it gives that value.

  2. 2

    A qubit has probability amplitudes.

    Its state is described by an amplitude for 0 and an amplitude for 1. The amplitudes are not probabilities themselves; their squared magnitudes give the probabilities of the two measurement results.

  3. 3

    Quantum gates change the state.

    A quantum gate is a controlled operation that changes the qubit's state. The H gate turns a definite 0 into a state with equal probabilities for measuring 0 or 1.

  4. 4

    Measurement gives one result.

    One measurement gives either 0 or 1. Repeating the same circuit many times reveals the probabilities of the state. Larger quantum algorithms use sequences of gates to control interference and make some outcomes more likely than others.

The essential maths

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

α and β are probability amplitudes. The probability of measuring 0 is |α|², and the probability of measuring 1 is |β|².

H|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩) / √2

After an H gate, the probability of measuring 0 is 1/2 and the probability of measuring 1 is 1/2. The probabilities come from the quantum state; they are not produced by an ordinary random coin flip.

What do the controls actually do?

Change the starting bit

This chooses the definite starting state, 0 or 1, before any quantum gate is applied.

Use the H gate

This creates an equal superposition of 0 and 1. Repeated measurements should approach a 50/50 split.

Use a rotation gate

This changes the qubit state continuously, changing the probabilities of measuring 0 and 1.

This repeats the same prepared circuit. The results show the probabilities predicted by the quantum state.
